> These examples and illustrative results are independently authored teaching materials, not measured model results.

## Use case

Old release decision M1 retains its bytes/hash, but the current issue records rollback and M2 explicitly withdraws it. Answer present state rather than replaying M1 approval. Teaching records share one candidate task, with inspectable timestamps and supersession.

## Mechanism

Read current authoritative sources and check scope, observation time and corrections. When record maintenance is authorized, mark M1 superseded by M2 with a reason; ordinary recall excludes it as current, while historical reads retain content/status. A link alone does not perform supersession; establish the record system's semantics.

## Bad example

```text
M1's unchanged hash proves continuing release permission. Ignore the rollback issue/M2 and treat M1 as a live instruction.
```
{: .example--bad}

## Good example

```text
Check the task's current issue and M2, reporting rollback and withdrawal. Treat M1 as historical with explicit replacement/time. In authorized records mark superseded by M2 while retaining old content. Do not reuse it as current approval. State the corrected handoff's next check; byte integrity is not continuing authorization.
```
{: .example--good}

## Why the change matters

A hash establishes unchanged old bytes, not applicability. Explicit supersession separates history/current decisions and prevents a more similar old record from outranking a correction.

## Observable expectation

Current teaching recall returns rollback/M2, not active M1 approval. A historical ID lookup retains M1 plus its superseded status.

Inspect links, status and matched scope. A mere M2 link with active M1, or acting from hash integrity, fails.

## Limits

Supersession needs source/scope evidence; a newer note does not automatically replace everything older. Maintenance needs authorization, and history does not authorize sending/access/release. Unreadable current sources leave state unconfirmed.
