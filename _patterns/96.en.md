> These examples and illustrative results are independently authored teaching materials, not measured model results.

## Use case

Upgrade shared core with service-a/worker-b consumers. Both depend on core; service-a has higher teaching risk. A current build baseline and preexisting local edits must be preserved. Changing everything and retrying full builds hides failure attribution.

## Mechanism

Record dependencies, risks, baseline and protected scope. Respect prerequisites, then prioritize higher-risk ready consumers. Change isolatable batches and run covering checks. Preserve failed states before continuing dependent work. Any rollback affects identifiable authorized batch edits only, not unrelated work.

## Bad example

```text
Change all three at once and rerun full builds indefinitely. Remove failing consumers from the list and call remaining passes a complete migration.
```
{: .example--bad}

## Good example

```text
Check core first, then higher-risk service-a, then worker-b, recording baseline/changes/results per batch. Do not advance over a failed prerequisite. Stop unchanged repetitive repair and report it while keeping the diff. Roll back only isolatable authorized batch changes, preserving existing edits. Account for every consumer, including failed/uninspected ones.
```
{: .example--good}

## Why the change matters

Bounded batches connect failures to changes; dependency ordering prevents downstream claims over broken prerequisites. Risk ordering validates important ready paths while the ledger exposes incomplete coverage.

## Observable expectation

An illustrative ledger is core passed, service-a failed, worker-b pending: migration is incomplete. If core fails, neither dependent may claim new-version verification.

Inspect version-matched evidence, retained gaps and unrelated edits preserved during rollback.

## Limits

Risk cannot bypass real prerequisites. Independent continuation depends on actual resource/dependency constraints. Builds alone do not establish business compatibility. Batch choices are teaching conventions; the source remains unconfirmed.
